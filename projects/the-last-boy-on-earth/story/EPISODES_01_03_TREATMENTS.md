# THE LAST BOY ON EARTH
## Episodes 1–3 Dramatic Treatments v0.1

**Status:** Development manuscript  
**Format:** 10 episodes × approximately 15 minutes  
**Canon rule:** This document expands the locked Season One map without locking the boy's name, exact age, survival mechanism, alien names or final disappearance mythology.

---

# Episode 1 — The Signal

## Episode purpose

Introduce the emotional promise of the series: a child who has survived by expecting nobody is finally found by beings who crossed the stars expecting everyone. The episode must deliver awe, tension and tenderness before exposition.

## Opening image

Deep space. The Voyager Golden Record drifts in blackness, impossibly small against the stars. Its etched instructions catch a distant sun. We hear fragments of Earth: surf, a heartbeat, laughter, music, greetings in many languages. The sounds do not form a nostalgic montage. They feel like a message still travelling after its makers have vanished.

An alien expedition vessel receives the record's pattern as a weak, repeating signal. The crew has followed it across an immense distance. Their approach is careful, reverent and scientific. They expect a civilisation capable of sending a message beyond its own solar system.

Earth turns below them, blue and green, visibly alive.

No cities shine.

## Sequence 1: Arrival

The expedition enters orbit and scans the planet. The ship intelligence identifies vast human structures but no active global network, no orbital traffic and no recognised civilisation-scale energy use. The young scientist argues that the biosphere itself may be masking lower-level activity. The security officer treats the silence as a possible threat. The expedition leader orders a limited descent.

The approach reveals the visual thesis of the series. Nature has not simply covered human civilisation. It has entered into it. Forests fill streets. Water runs through transport tunnels. Towers stand like cliffs inhabited by birds. Bridges have become hanging gardens. The city is recognisable but transformed enough to feel archaeological.

The crew lands outside the dense urban centre. Their vessel is not a generic saucer. Its form unfolds with the logic established in the alien design grammar: layered, memory-like surfaces opening to reveal translucent structural seams. Its landing creates almost no violence in the environment.

The crew steps onto Earth.

Their first reaction is not fear. It is grief for people they never knew.

## Sequence 2: Evidence of one

The expedition follows small anomalies that do not match natural decay. A cut rope. A sharpened tool. A rain collector recently repaired. A path through wet grass. The security officer believes the signs could be automated remnants or an animal displaying learned behaviour. The young scientist sees intentionality everywhere.

Inside an abandoned public building, the crew finds a wall covered in hand-drawn maps. Certain areas are crossed out. Others are marked with dates, weather symbols and brief warnings. The writing is human, recent and inconsistent in height, suggesting it was made over several years by someone growing taller.

A sound moves above them.

The boy watches from a hidden position. We do not yet see him clearly. He studies each crew member, their tools and their relationship to one another. His intelligence is immediately practical. He notices the leader gives commands, the security officer checks exits, the scientist touches everything and the ship intelligence speaks through a small mobile interface.

He steals one of their scanning devices.

The theft triggers a brief pursuit through the building and out into the reclaimed city. The boy uses routes he has prepared: suspended cables, narrowed passages and warning chimes made from human objects. The aliens are technologically superior but environmentally clumsy. He is not presented as superhuman. He succeeds because this is his home and because he has planned for danger his entire life.

## Sequence 3: The trap

The security officer corners the boy in a flooded transit concourse. We see him fully for the first time: approximately eleven to thirteen, weathered clothing repeatedly repaired, alert eyes, a bag of carefully selected tools and small objects. He holds the stolen scanner as both weapon and shield.

Communication fails. The crew's translation system has only recordings of historical human language and cannot immediately map his speech patterns. The scientist plays fragments from the Golden Record, hoping to demonstrate peaceful intent.

The boy freezes when the music begins.

This is the first clue that the record is personally meaningful to him. He knows the sound. He knows more than he should.

The moment is broken by structural collapse. Water pressure and root growth give way beneath the concourse. The security officer has a clear opportunity to save the scanner or save the boy. They choose the boy without hesitation, taking the physical impact themselves.

The boy is shocked by the act. His worldview says strangers protect resources, missions and themselves. This stranger protected him.

## Sequence 4: First choice

The expedition leader orders the crew to lower tools and create distance. The young scientist places a simple object on the ground: a visual recording of the Golden Record's path through space. The boy approaches but refuses to release the scanner.

The leader speaks slowly. The translation is rough, but one phrase emerges:

“We came because your world called.”

The boy looks toward the ruined city, then at the stars, then back at them. He almost runs.

Instead, he returns the scanner.

It is the smallest possible act of trust, but it is active and costly for him.

## Ending hook

Back aboard the vessel, the ship intelligence isolates the boy's reaction to the music. His pulse changed before the familiar section began, suggesting anticipation rather than recognition after the fact.

The young scientist asks how he knew what was coming.

Cut to the boy in his hidden shelter. He opens a protected container containing a damaged human playback device. He activates it.

The same music plays.

Beside it is a hand-drawn map filled with repeating signal marks.

## Character turns

- **The Boy:** chooses not to run after witnessing an act of protection.
- **Young Scientist:** shifts from historic excitement to concern for a living person.
- **Security Officer:** instinctively values the boy over mission equipment, contradicting their stated suspicion.
- **Expedition Leader:** accepts that discovery has created immediate responsibility.

## Visual set pieces

1. Golden Record crossing deep space.
2. First orbital reveal of a living Earth without civilisation lights.
3. Alien vessel descending over reclaimed Dublin.
4. Pursuit through a flooded, overgrown public building.
5. First full portrait of the boy in the transit concourse.
6. Golden Record music stopping him in place.
7. Final reveal of the boy's private signal map.

## Production note

The episode should remain approximately 70% intimate mystery and 30% epic scale. The largest visual images should make the quiet human moments feel more vulnerable, not compete with them.

# Episode 2 — The Visitors

## Episode purpose

Turn first contact into the beginning of a relationship. The episode is built around the idea that trust is not created by declarations but by small, observable acts: sharing food, respecting space, returning an object and admitting uncertainty.

## Opening situation

The boy wakes before dawn and prepares for the crew's return. He resets warning lines, hides supplies and moves emotionally important objects to a safer location. He has survived by assuming that anything discovered can be taken.

The expedition leader debates whether approaching him again will help or frighten him deeper into hiding. The young scientist argues that abandoning him after first contact would repeat the very loss they are trying to understand. The security officer insists on boundaries: no following him to his shelter, no invasive scans and no removal of human material without consent.

This surprising position reveals that the security officer's protectiveness is already extending toward the boy.

## Sequence 1: A bad introduction

The crew returns to the meeting place carrying carefully chosen offerings. Their choices reveal how little they understand humanity. The scientist brings a perfectly preserved digital archive of agricultural information. The leader brings a universal medical tool. The security officer brings a sealed container of clean water.

The boy ignores all three and focuses on a simple heat source used to warm food.

The translation system improves by analysing his previous speech, but it remains incomplete. The scientist asks too many questions too quickly: name, family, population, age, health, origin, knowledge of the signal. The boy interprets the questions as interrogation and leaves.

The scientist follows, violating the agreed boundary.

The boy triggers a harmless but humiliating trap that drops the scientist into an old decorative fountain filled with rainwater and plants. The scene provides comedy without making either character foolish. He had warned them through signs they could not read. They ignored the boundary.

## Sequence 2: The meal

Later, the boy returns because he sees smoke from the crew's failed attempt to prepare local food. The aliens can analyse its molecular structure but misunderstand the practical sequence of making it edible. He intervenes to prevent them ruining a scarce resource.

The resulting meal becomes the episode's centrepiece. Nobody can yet communicate fluently. Meaning emerges through demonstration, imitation and mistakes.

The young scientist copies the boy too exactly, including gestures that are not part of eating. The security officer attempts to cut food with a precision tool and accidentally produces hundreds of identical pieces. The ship intelligence interprets laughter as a distress response until the boy laughs again.

For the first time, the boy relaxes enough to eat in front of others.

The leader waits until he has taken the first portion before allowing the crew to eat, signalling that the food is his and his consent matters. He notices.

## Sequence 3: What not to take

The scientist discovers a small human object attached to the boy's bag. Its purpose is unclear, but its wear suggests constant use. The scientist reaches toward it. The boy reacts sharply and backs away.

Instead of defending the scientist, the leader asks them to apologise. The translation system cannot yet carry the emotional nuance, so the scientist returns the object without scanning it and places their own most personal field token beside it as collateral.

The boy studies the token. It contains layered records from the scientist's home culture: voices, images and tactile memory patterns. He understands that it matters.

He returns it untouched.

This becomes their first reciprocal act of respect.

## Sequence 4: The map

The boy allows the crew into a neutral space he uses as a workroom, but not his actual home. Its walls reveal years of observations: weather cycles, animal routes, structural hazards and signal marks. The crew realises he is not merely surviving day to day. He has built a disciplined knowledge system.

The ship intelligence overlays his marks with orbital data. Most correspond to natural or degraded human transmissions. Several do not.

They repeat at irregular intervals from different points across the city.

The boy indicates that he has followed some of them before and stopped. The translated reason arrives in fragments:

“Voice... not voice. Knows... when I listen.”

The crew initially interprets this as fear or superstition. Then the ship intelligence confirms that one signal changed timing after the boy activated his receiver.

Something may have responded to him.

## Ending hook

The young scientist asks whether the boy is alone.

He does not answer.

Instead, he unfolds a larger map. Several locations are crossed out. One remains circled.

A new mark has appeared there since the previous night.

## Character turns

- **The Boy:** permits the crew into a controlled part of his life and shares information he has protected.
- **Young Scientist:** learns that curiosity without consent can become harm.
- **Expedition Leader:** establishes a moral rule that the boy is a person, not a discovery.
- **Security Officer:** becomes the clearest advocate for practical boundaries.
- **Ship Intelligence:** encounters laughter as an emotional behaviour that cannot be reduced to alarm data.

## Visual set pieces

1. Dawn preparations in the boy's hidden survival network.
2. The scientist falling into an overgrown fountain.
3. Shared meal beneath a collapsed glass roof filled with morning light.
4. Exchange of the boy's keepsake and the scientist's memory token.
5. Signal map merging with alien orbital projection.
6. Final close-up on the newly active location.

## Later consequences created

- The leader's consent rule will make the later sealed mission orders feel like a personal betrayal.
- The scientist's memory token can become a recurring symbol of friendship.
- The responsive signal establishes that the boy's isolation may have been observed or managed.
- The crossed-out map locations provide future episode destinations and evidence of previous failed searches.

# Episode 3 — Museum of Everything

## Episode purpose

Use comedy, archaeology and grief to demonstrate the series engine. Human objects are not valuable because they survived. They are valuable because relationships once gave them meaning. The episode also provides the first credible physical evidence that another intelligent presence has moved through Earth recently.

## Opening situation

The active map location corresponds to a major museum complex. The boy refuses to go. His reaction is stronger than simple fear. The building is connected to a memory he has avoided.

The crew can reach it without him, but their ability to interpret human spaces remains limited. The leader offers a choice rather than an order. They will investigate, but he decides whether to join.

He follows at a distance.

## Sequence 1: Wrong meanings

The museum has become a breathtaking indoor ecosystem. Sections of roof are open to the sky. Trees grow through exhibition halls. Rainwater moves along former visitor routes. Animals have nested inside monumental displays.

The aliens begin cataloguing objects and repeatedly assign plausible but incorrect functions:

- a trophy is classified as a ritual drinking vessel;
- a child's pushchair is interpreted as a mobile shrine;
- a vending machine is treated as a sealed food archive of high ceremonial status;
- a sports mascot costume is identified as evidence of human-animal political leadership.

The comedy comes from disciplined alien logic applied to incomplete evidence. The boy initially refuses to correct them. Their certainty irritates him, but it also draws him into conversation.

When the scientist confidently misidentifies a simple toy, the boy finally demonstrates its real use. The scientist is delighted rather than embarrassed. This changes the emotional temperature. Knowledge can be shared without becoming a test.

## Sequence 2: The value test

A structural warning forces the group to choose what to protect from a collapsing display area. The security officer prioritises a compact device that may contain technical data. The boy risks himself to retrieve a small, visibly ordinary object.

The object has almost no material or historical importance. Its emotional importance is immense.

The boy cannot yet explain its full connection to his past. He only says that somebody held it when they were afraid.

The security officer returns the technical device to danger and helps save the object instead. This is not sentimental surrender. It is the first time they accept the boy's definition of value over the mission's.

## Sequence 3: Memory room

The museum contains an installation built from recorded human voices. Environmental damage has caused the system to activate unpredictably. As the crew enters, fragments of testimony, jokes, family stories and ordinary observations speak from different parts of the darkened room.

The aliens experience human memory not as ordered data but as overlapping presence. Their culture's respect for shared memory makes the room especially powerful. The ship intelligence attempts to isolate and classify each voice. The scientist asks it not to. For once, they listen without analysing.

The boy hears a voice or phrase connected to his past. The exact identity remains deliberately unconfirmed because his family history is not yet locked. His composure breaks. He leaves the room.

The young scientist follows but stops at a respectful distance. Instead of questioning him, they activate their own memory token and allow a faint sound from home to play. The message is simple: I also miss someone.

The boy sits beside them.

## Sequence 4: Evidence of another visitor

During the return route, the security officer notices that a large display object has been moved recently. Root growth and dust patterns prove it shifted after the surrounding area stabilised. The boy did not move it. The object is too heavy for local animals.

Behind it is a narrow access route containing three clues:

1. a clean cut through a material the boy cannot cut;
2. a temporary marker made from a non-human compound;
3. a footprint or pressure pattern that does not match the expedition crew's anatomy or equipment.

The discovery is handled quietly. No attacker appears. The evidence is more unsettling because it is deliberate and recent.

The expedition leader orders the information restricted until they understand whether it represents danger. The ship intelligence obeys but records that the order conflicts with the crew's new commitment to openness with the boy.

The first seed of the later mission betrayal is planted.

## Ending hook

The boy finds one additional detail the aliens missed: a human object placed upright in the access route, protected from the weather.

It was not dropped.

It was left to be found.

He turns it over. A fresh mark has been added to it, matching one of the symbols on his signal map.

## Character turns

- **The Boy:** allows another person to witness his grief without retreating completely into isolation.
- **Young Scientist:** chooses companionship over investigation.
- **Security Officer:** protects emotional value rather than strategic value.
- **Expedition Leader:** makes the first secrecy decision that may later damage trust.
- **Ship Intelligence:** recognises a conflict between instruction, truth and relationship.

## Visual set pieces

1. Museum atrium transformed into an indoor forest.
2. Alien misinterpretation montage of ordinary human objects.
3. Rescue of the emotionally valuable object during structural collapse.
4. Dark memory installation filled with disembodied human voices.
5. Boy and young scientist sitting apart but connected by two worlds of memory.
6. Recently moved monumental display revealing a hidden route.
7. Final marked object left intentionally for the boy.

## Episode engine demonstrated

- **Physical discovery:** the museum and evidence of another visitor.
- **Emotional discovery:** objects matter because of relationships.
- **Alien misunderstanding:** human artefacts cannot be understood from function alone.
- **Boy's challenged belief:** grief shared with another person does not erase the person he lost.
- **Mystery advance:** someone else has recently moved through Earth and may know his signal language.
- **Relationship change:** the boy and scientist become friends rather than observer and subject.

# Continuity and canon review

## Preserved locked canon

- Ten episodes of approximately fifteen minutes.
- The expedition follows the Voyager Golden Record to Earth.
- Earth is alive, overgrown and reclaimed by nature.
- The boy is the last known human child, not definitively the final human.
- Friendship with the young scientist is the emotional heart.
- The security officer's mistrust evolves toward protection.
- Evidence of another recent visitor appears in Episode 3.
- The larger disappearance mythology remains unresolved.

## Deliberately provisional

- Exact Dublin buildings and museum identity.
- Exact human language used in first contact.
- The boy's personal keepsake and family connection.
- The scientist's memory-token design.
- The form of the non-human trace in Episode 3.
- Whether the responsive signals are human, alien or automated.

## Required later payoffs

1. The boy's early knowledge of the Golden Record must be causally explained.
2. The responsive signal must connect to the Episode 5 message addressed to him.
3. The third-party trace must connect to the approaching force, off-world humans or hidden technology.
4. The leader's first secrecy decision must contribute to the Episode 7 collapse of trust.
5. The scientist's memory token should return during a later separation or sacrifice.
6. The ordinary object saved in Episode 3 should reappear when the boy decides what humanity is worth preserving.

## Manuscript readiness

These treatments are long-form source material for approximately six to eight designed proposal pages, depending on the final image-to-copy ratio. They are suitable for adaptation into:

- three episode spreads in the Greenlight Book;

- dialogue-outline development;
- the two-minute proof-of-concept script.

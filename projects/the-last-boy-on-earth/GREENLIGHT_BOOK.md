# PAGE 02 — THE IMAGE THAT STARTED IT

## We sent a record into the dark.

In 1977, humanity launched a message beyond the Solar System: sounds, images, music, greetings and evidence that life on Earth had once looked outward and wondered who might be listening.

Long after the people who made it have vanished from Earth, that invitation is finally found.

An expedition follows it home expecting to meet a civilisation.

They find one child.

**Editorial note:** the Voyager Golden Record is the factual inspiration; the future discovery and expedition are fictional premises.

# PAGE 03 — THE PREMISE

## They crossed the stars to meet humanity. They arrived too late.

**One-line premise**  
An alien expedition follows humanity’s Voyager Golden Record to Earth and discovers that our civilisation has disappeared, leaving behind a single human boy.

**Series logline**  
When a young alien scientist befriends the last child living on a reclaimed Earth, their search for what happened to humanity exposes a secret inside the expedition itself and forces both children to decide whether the past should be preserved, obeyed or answered.

**Format**  
10 episodes × approximately 15 minutes  
Premium family science-fiction adventure

# PAGE 04 — SERIES OVERVIEW

## First contact happens after everyone is gone.

An expedition from a distant civilisation reaches Earth after discovering humanity’s Golden Record. They expect cities full of people. Instead they find a planet transformed by ecological return and one human child who has learned to survive among the quiet remains.

The youngest member of the expedition, a brilliant and emotionally direct scientist, becomes fascinated by him. He is equally fascinated by someone who knows nothing about Earth and refuses to treat its ordinary objects as ordinary. Their friendship turns exploration into a two-way exchange: he teaches the visitors how to read the world that remains; they show him that humanity’s voice travelled farther than anyone imagined.

But the expedition did not come to Earth with the whole truth. As dormant systems wake, human signals begin to surface and the crew’s sealed mission collides with the children’s growing trust. The mystery stops being simply *Where did everyone go?* and becomes a harder question: *Who gets to decide what a civilisation is allowed to remember?*

**Image:** first encounter wide, curiosity and uncertainty carrying the tension.

---

# PAGE 05 — WHY THIS STORY

## Being remembered is not the same as being known.

The Golden Record is an extraordinary act of optimism: a civilisation introducing itself to strangers it may never meet. But a record can only preserve what someone chose to put on it.

*The Last Boy on Earth* begins with that beautiful limitation.

The visitors arrive knowing humanity through an artefact. The boy knows humanity through absence, habits, rooms, recordings, mistakes and the stubborn traces of ordinary lives. Neither version is complete.

The series is about what happens when an archive meets a witness.

Under the science-fiction mystery is a simple emotional idea for children and adults: we do not become real to one another by collecting information. We become real through attention, trust and the willingness to listen when the answer complicates the story we expected to hear.

**Image:** Golden Record reflected in the boy’s eye, alien hand nearby but not touching him.

---

# PAGE 06 — THE EMOTIONAL PROMISE

## A mystery with wonder in its bones.

This is not a story about wandering through a dead world. Earth is alive. The comedy comes from first contact, the suspense from what has been hidden, and the emotion from two young people discovering that each possesses a piece of the world the other cannot reach alone.

**Wonder:** seeing rain, kitchens, birds, traffic lights and bedrooms through alien eyes.  
**Loneliness:** the quiet truth beneath the adventure: one child has had no equal companion.  
**Humour:** confident alien interpretations of ordinary human behaviour.  
**Suspense:** signals, sealed orders and waking infrastructure steadily reveal that humanity’s absence is not the end of the story.  
**Belonging:** friendship grows through permission, mistakes, teaching and repair.  
**Hope:** the season moves from an old message sent into silence toward a new message capable of receiving an answer.

The first Golden Record said: *This is who we were.*  
The new one asks: *Who are we to each other now?*

**Image:** two children beneath a vast night sky sharing one listening device.

# PAGE 07 — EARTH IS ALIVE

## The planet survived us.

Earth is not a dead world. It is a living world from which organised human civilisation has vanished.

The emotional power of the setting comes from two truths held at the same time: humanity is absent, and human life is still visible everywhere.

A school timetable remains beneath moss. A kitchen table sits under a collapsed roof. A tram line has become a wetland. Trees grow through offices whose screens are long dark. These places are not generic apocalypse scenery. They are evidence of interrupted lives.

Nature is not the antagonist and not decorative dressing. Water, roots, weather, animals and seasonal growth have reorganised the planet. The world should create wonder before danger.

**Image:** sweeping aerial of reclaimed Dublin at soft Atlantic golden hour.

---

# PAGE 08 — RECLAIMED DUBLIN

## A city built on stories becomes a city made of memory.

Dublin remains recognisable through the River Liffey, bridges, Georgian geometry, civic buildings, weathered stone and compressed streets. But its human systems have been replaced by ecological ones.

The series avoids endless rubble and ash. Reclamation is shown through roots widening old seams, rain entering broken roofs, birds occupying towers, reeds filling transit routes, salt reshaping exposed structures and plants following light through architecture.

The preferred visual baseline is late spring moving toward early summer: wet greens, slate skies, old brick, rusted metal, sudden shafts of warm light and long evenings.

Dublin matters because it is intimate enough to walk and culturally synonymous with stories. The city itself becomes an argument that memory survives through places as much as archives.

**Image:** Liffey corridor transformed by vegetation, water and quick-changing weather.

# PAGE 09 — FOUR LOCATION FAMILIES

## One city. Four repeatable worlds.

**The Vertical City** — rooftops, towers, hanging vegetation and improvised crossings. This is where the boy’s environmental competence becomes authority.

**The River City** — Liffey, canals, flooded transit routes and tidal infrastructure. Water creates beauty, danger and evidence.

**Memory Interiors** — museums, kitchens, bedrooms, libraries and voice archives where ordinary human life remains emotionally present.

**Hidden Systems** — archives, shelters, signal nodes and broadcast infrastructure deliberately concealed or maintained after civilisation disappeared.

These four families give the series visual variety while keeping production design coherent and reusable.

**Image:** four cinematic panels, not a technical diagram.

# PAGE 10 — THE VERTICAL CITY

## Survival knowledge becomes character authority.

The boy uses linked roofs, maintenance ladders, improvised crossings and higher observation points to move above unstable streets. What appears accidental to alien instruments is actually a survival network accumulated through years of observation.

The aliens possess more advanced technology but less local knowledge. They initially treat his routes as inefficient. Over time, they learn that he is reading nesting behaviour, changing water marks, stressed roots, wind and structural sound — information their scans do not automatically understand.

This visual space supports epic scale without turning the series into action spectacle. A rooftop crossing should always reveal something about trust, hierarchy or the boy’s relationship with home.

**Image:** boy leading the alien crew across a dangerous vegetated upper route.

---

# PAGE 11 — THE RIVER CITY

## Water has rewritten the map.

The Liffey, canals, flooded tunnels and drainage routes now form a connected ecology through the city.

The boy reads tide lines, floating debris, bird behaviour and changes in water colour. The crew can map the river from orbit, but cannot replace local timing.

Old signs, benches, bicycles and public architecture remain along the quays as emotional traces. Hazards come from cold, current, unstable walls, changing weather and hidden infrastructure rather than invented monsters.

Water also carries clues. Objects move downstream. Signals travel through damaged civic systems. Routes appear and vanish twice a day.

**Image:** misted Ha’penny Bridge and an improvised crossing below it.

---

# PAGE 12 — MEMORY INTERIORS

## Civilisations disappear at human scale.

The most powerful human remains are not monuments. They are bedrooms, cupboards, school recordings, kitchens, handwritten lists, repaired toys and worn furniture.

The aliens initially classify these spaces as archaeology. The boy experiences them as incomplete relationships.

A height mark on a wall can tell more about humanity than a ruined tower. A cracked mug can matter because somebody held it during a difficult morning. A voice archive can be both irresistible to the alien crew and almost unbearable to the boy.

Every object should do one clear dramatic job: function as a tool, a trace, a memory or a clue. Nothing exists only to decorate the world.

**Image:** quiet domestic room entered by alien visitors, interrupted life visible in small details.

---

# PAGE 13 — HIDDEN SYSTEMS

## Someone expected Earth to answer.

Below the reclaimed city are archives, shelters, signal nodes and technology spaces that were intentionally maintained or concealed.

They do not look like pristine futuristic laboratories. They are human-built, repaired, compromised by time and often integrated into ordinary civic infrastructure.

This is where the season shifts from archaeology to active mystery. A relay changes timing after the boy listens. An archive opens specifically for him. A domestic survival network proves somebody planned for his future. A broadcast system still knows how to reach beyond Earth.

The key question is not whether old machines are still working. It is why some of them appear to be waiting.

**Image:** dormant infrastructure waking beneath roots, flood marks and aged concrete.

---

# PAGE 14 — THE MEMORY ARK

## Recommended mythology framework — pending final canon lock.

The strongest current causal system is a distributed cultural-continuity network designed to prevent humanity from being reduced to a single authorised version of itself.

The Memory Ark is not one bunker or one supercomputer. It is a web of homes, libraries, schools, hospitals, civic systems and off-world routes preserving context, testimony, contradictions and the ability for later humans to reconstruct relationships between records.

Its dramatic value is thematic: memory becomes a contested public resource rather than a magical plot device.

The boy is not a biological key or chosen one. His importance is that he is a living witness capable of judgement, consent and response.

**Image:** layered human archive activating around boy and scientist. Layout must label this mythology as provisional.

# PAGE 15 — THE OLD RECORD / THE NEW RECORD

## The first message was an introduction. The second becomes a conversation.

The Voyager Golden Record is a fixed artefact. It says, in effect: *this is what we chose to show you about ourselves.*

The season gradually exposes the limits of that model. A fixed archive can be protected, stolen, curated or rewritten. A living culture cannot be represented honestly by one keeper forever.

The finale therefore evolves the Golden Record from an object into a principle: voices that can reply, disagree, correct and add to the story.

The visual motif moves from one gold disc crossing darkness to a distributed constellation of living transmissions.

**Image:** gold disc contrasted with many human voices becoming a constellation.

# PAGE 16 — THE BOY

## Capable enough to survive. Young enough to still need somebody.

The last known human child is the emotional centre of the series. He is approximately 11–13, with twelve the recommended playing age pending final lock.

He is fiercely capable in an empty world, emotionally unpractised with other people and quietly desperate to learn that belonging does not always end in loss.

He reads weather, terrain and urban danger quickly. He can repair systems he has maintained before. He records messages for unknown listeners and collects ordinary human objects because memory has become part of survival.

His contradiction is the heart of the show: he can move through a reclaimed city with confidence, yet apology, play, dependence and trust feel dangerous.

**Image:** intimate 85mm portrait, repaired practical clothing, direct but not heroic gaze.

---

# PAGE 17 — SURVIVAL IS CHARACTER

## He survives through systems, not superpowers.

The boy uses a network of shelters, caches, observation points and maintenance rooms rather than one magical bunker. Water comes from rain, surviving infrastructure and purification. Food comes from growing spaces, preserved stores and environmental knowledge. Energy is local and small-scale: salvaged solar, hand charging, batteries and residual civic power.

His competence is pattern-based and learned. He cannot rebuild advanced technology from first principles, fight like a soldier or survive indefinitely without infrastructure.

The most important system he maintains is psychological. He records daily logs, marks maps, catalogues objects and stores unanswered transmissions. These records preserve the possibility that someone may eventually listen.

**Recommended signature object:** a repaired pocket audio recorder. Final approval pending.

**Image:** boy performing a practised water, power or weather routine.

# PAGE 18 — THE YOUNG SCIENTIST

## Knows thousands of facts about humanity. Understands almost none of the important ones.

The young alien scientist is the boy’s first true friend and the audience’s second lens on Earth.

Raised in a culture that treats memory as a shared public duty, the scientist grew up regarding the Golden Record as an unanswered invitation. Their curiosity is sincere, intellectually fearless and sometimes intrusive.

The scientist’s season arc is from observation to relationship. At first the boy is evidence. Later he becomes the person whose consent matters more than the answer to any question.

The humour comes from confident misunderstandings of ordinary human objects, customs and emotions. The heart comes from the fact that the scientist occasionally understands the boy more clearly than the adults do.

**Image:** humane but unmistakably non-human portrait, culturally specific textile and recording tool.

---

# PAGE 19 — THE EXPEDITION LEADER

## Discovery creates responsibility.

The expedition leader begins as a disciplined custodian of mission ethics: cautious, calm and convinced that good institutions exist to prevent personal bias from endangering others.

Earth breaks that certainty.

The leader gradually shifts from protecting a historic discovery to protecting a child. The conflict is not between rules and recklessness. It is between protocol that claims moral legitimacy and the immediate responsibility created by a living person standing in front of you.

By the final act, the leader understands that obedience can become a way of avoiding ownership of a decision.

**Image:** portrait divided between Earth light and controlled mission interior light.

---

# PAGE 20 — THE CREW

## One civilisation. Four different answers to humanity.

**Young Scientist:** humanity is a culture waiting to be understood.  
**Expedition Leader:** humanity is a vanished civilisation whose record must be responsibly closed.  
**Security Officer:** humanity may have created the danger that ended it.  
**Ship Intelligence:** humanity is the sum of its surviving records.

The boy breaks all four assumptions because he is alive, contradictory, funny, frightened, practical and capable of disagreeing with their interpretation.

The crew should read as one culture in anatomy, material and gesture, but each role must be distinct in silhouette and movement. Their civilisation expresses authority through responsibility rather than decoration, and their technology should feel inherited, repaired and handled rather than magically pristine.

**Image:** group inside the ship, distinct silhouettes, no militarised pose.

---

# PAGE 21 — THE FRIENDSHIP ENGINE

## Each child knows something the other cannot learn alone.

The boy understands Earth through lived context. The scientist understands the larger universe and the fact that humanity’s message travelled beyond the planet.

Their friendship grows through exchange rather than rescue.

He teaches the scientist how to read rain, objects, rooms and silences. The scientist teaches him that his world is not the whole world — and that curiosity can point forward instead of only backward.

Every episode should include an exchange in which one of them initially has the advantage, gets something wrong and gives the other person room to matter.

Trust is built through small acts: asking before touching, returning a private object unscanned, sharing food, admitting uncertainty, choosing a person over mission equipment.

**Image:** boy teaching an Earth skill while scientist shares alien listening technology.

---

# PAGE 22 — RELATIONSHIP MAP

## The season is a map of changing permission.

**Boy ↔ Scientist:** observation becomes friendship, friendship fractures under hidden mission truth, then returns as chosen moral alliance.

**Boy ↔ Leader:** discovery becomes guardianship, guardianship becomes control, and finally protection becomes the granting of real agency.

**Boy ↔ Security Officer:** mutual suspicion turns into practical trust and physical protection.

**Boy ↔ Ship Intelligence:** lived memory confronts recorded memory; each possesses what the other lacks.

**Crew ↔ Command:** institutional legitimacy is tested against lived consequence.

**Everyone ↔ Absent Humanity:** the question changes from *what were they?* to *who has the right to answer for them now?*

**Image:** editorial portraits using distance, eyelines and shared objects rather than corporate arrows.

# PAGE 23 — SEASON ONE

## Ten chapters. One growing act of trust.

Season One begins with an impossible arrival and ends with an answer humanity could never have sent from the Voyager spacecraft: a living voice.

Across ten approximately 15-minute episodes, the story expands in three movements.

**CONTACT** — an alien expedition follows the Golden Record to a living but silent Earth. They discover a single boy and begin to understand humanity through the ordinary things it left behind.

**THE SIGNAL** — a repeating human transmission, a protected archive and the place the boy once called home reveal that his survival may not have been accidental.

**THE CHOICE** — a concealed alien mission turns friendship into betrayal. Evidence of surviving human communities beyond Earth changes the meaning of the boy’s loneliness, while an approaching force makes memory itself dangerous.

**Season movement:** Arrival → Contact → Exploration → Signal → Archive → Home → Betrayal → Broadcast → Choice → Answer.

# PAGE 24 — EPISODES 1–3: CONTACT

### Episode 1 — Working title: THE SIGNAL
The expedition reaches Earth after following the legacy of the Voyager Golden Record. They find a breathtaking world reclaimed by nature and evidence that one person is moving through the ruins. A pursuit becomes first contact when a crew member chooses to save the boy rather than mission equipment. That night, they discover he already knows the music on the Golden Record.

### Episode 2 — THE VISITORS
First contact becomes coexistence. A failed introduction, shared meal and exchange of personal objects teach both sides that trust is built through boundaries and small acts. The boy eventually reveals a hand-drawn map of repeating signals, one of which appears to have changed after he listened.

### Episode 3 — MUSEUM OF EVERYTHING
The crew repeatedly misreads ordinary human artefacts. Comedy becomes grief as the boy shows them that value cannot be measured by rarity alone. Evidence of recent intelligent movement changes the expedition’s question from “What happened here?” to “Who is still acting?”

# PAGE 25 — EPISODE 1: THE SIGNAL

## The world called. Someone answered.

The expedition reaches a blue, green and silent Earth. On the ground, tiny signs reveal recent human intention: a repaired rain collector, cut rope, sharpened tool, maps marked by a hand that has grown older over time.

Then someone steals a scanner.

The boy knows the city better than their instruments do. A pursuit through flooded infrastructure ends in collapse. A crew member chooses to save him rather than retrieve the equipment. The boy returns the scanner but refuses to join them.

That night, the crew analyses his reaction to music from the Golden Record. He did not merely recognise it. He anticipated it.

**Cliffhanger:** why does he know a message sent into space centuries before?

---

# PAGE 26 — EPISODE 2: THE VISITORS

## Trust begins with what you do not take.

The visitors return with gifts and immediately misunderstand what the boy values. Translation improves, but behaviour matters more than vocabulary.

A failed meal gives them their first shared laugh. When the scientist reaches for a worn object attached to the boy’s bag, he recoils. The scientist returns it without scanning and leaves an alien memory token as collateral.

Only then does the boy show the crew his map.

Orbital data confirms several marked signals are real. One transmission appears to have changed timing after he listened.

**Cliffhanger:** a new mark appears overnight.

---

# PAGE 27 — EPISODE 3: MUSEUM OF EVERYTHING

## Objects survive. Meaning has to be carried.

A signal leads the group into a museum transformed into an ecosystem. The aliens catalogue human objects with absurd confidence and repeatedly get them wrong.

Their mistakes open the door to grief. The boy risks himself for a small ordinary object because somebody once held it when afraid. In a damaged installation, human voices overlap through darkness: jokes, family stories, testimony and unfinished thoughts.

The aliens finally stop analysing and simply listen.

Then the group finds evidence that another intelligence has entered the building recently.

**Question:** if the boy is alone, who else has been moving through the city?

---

# PAGE 28 — EPISODES 4–6: THE SIGNAL

### Episode 4 — THE CITY THAT GREW BACK
The boy proves that alien scanning cannot replace local knowledge. When he is trapped during a structural collapse, the leader sacrifices mission equipment to save him. He repairs a dormant relay by hand and a recent human transmission emerges. Across Dublin, distant lights answer.

### Episode 5 — A VOICE IN THE DARK
Coordinates lead to a protected archive of ordinary human life. Then a restricted system activates specifically for the boy and plays a damaged message addressed to him. He admits he has heard fragments before and stopped following them because hope made surviving harder.

### Episode 6 — HOME
The signal leads to the boy’s former home: a domestic survival network proving someone taught, protected and concealed him. A hidden exchange then reveals alien command knew enough about this place to keep information from the crew.

# PAGE 29 — EPISODE 4: THE CITY THAT GREW BACK

## The city becomes his proof.

The boy reads what scanners miss: nesting behaviour, changing water marks, stressed roots and the sound of structures settling. As he leads the crew across rooftops and buildings transformed into wetlands, the expedition hierarchy changes.

A living bridge of root and old infrastructure tears loose. Protocol says preserve the expedition. The leader chooses the boy.

At the relay, the boy restores a connection alien technology could not solve alone.

A human phrase begins repeating across the city.

Far away, lights answer.

---

# PAGE 30 — EPISODE 5: A VOICE IN THE DARK

## Hope is the dangerous thing.

The transmission hides coordinates beneath damaged language. They lead below Dublin into a protected archive containing private recordings, school voices, domestic video, recipes, jokes and unfinished conversations.

Inside a restricted chamber, the system responds to the boy’s presence.

A human message begins.

Most of it is damaged. One fact survives: it is addressed to him.

He admits he followed similar signals for years and eventually chose certainty over hope because hope made loneliness harder.

The archive reveals a route to a place connected to his past.

“I know where we have to go.”

---

# PAGE 31 — EPISODE 6: HOME

## Someone planned for him to continue.

The route to the boy’s former home is hidden by ordinary-looking obstacles: redirected water, thorn growth, false dead ends and collapsed walls.

Inside is a domestic survival network, not a fortress. Height marks climb a wall. Clothing was repaired in multiple sizes. Different voices recorded lessons. Water, food and power systems were designed for one growing child to understand.

Then the ship detects a fragment of its own mission architecture inside the human systems.

Alien command knew something about this place before the crew arrived.

The boy’s home becomes the point where two concealed plans meet.

---

# PAGE 32 — EPISODES 7–10: THE CHOICE

### Episode 7 — THE GOLDEN LIE
The crew decrypts sealed orders to locate and retrieve a terrestrial memory node before another power reaches it. Alien command knew a human survivor might be present. The boy sees the contradiction immediately: they loved humanity’s record because it could not disagree with them, yet arrived planning to take part of humanity’s memory without consent.

### Episode 8 — THE LAST BROADCAST
The boy activates part of the recommended Memory Ark framework and discovers scattered off-world human communities carrying incomplete fragments of their shared past. They are alive but disconnected. Some histories appear to have been standardised or erased.

### Episode 9 — DEPARTURE
The crew faces three dangerous choices: hide Earth’s memory, surrender it to authority, or reconnect human communities and expose the network. The boy finds a fourth option: create a new record made of living voices rather than a single owned archive.

### Episode 10 — A NEW RECORD
The climax is communication rather than conquest. The boy, crew and scattered communities assemble a distributed message no single civilisation can own. Earth transmits not a perfect definition of humanity, but an invitation to answer.

---

# PAGE 33 — EPISODE 7: THE GOLDEN LIE

## Being remembered is not the same as being allowed to speak.

The sealed mission file finally opens.

The expedition’s classified objective was to locate and retrieve a terrestrial memory node before a competing power could reach it. Command describes culture in strategic language: authentication, reconstruction capacity, cohesion, control.

A hidden human system reveals fragments of a distributed Memory Ark preserving contradictory versions of human life so no single archive can become the official truth.

Then command issues a direct order: secure the node, isolate the human survivor, prevent unauthorised transmission.

They knew he might be here.

The boy leaves before the crew decides what to do.

For him, their hesitation is enough.

---

# PAGE 34 — EPISODE 8: THE LAST BROADCAST

## Humanity survived. Just not together.

The boy reaches a broadcast complex linked to the hidden systems below Dublin and activates a star map.

Humanity is not gone.

Scattered settlements survive beyond Earth, each carrying incomplete pieces of a larger inheritance. Some retained language, some engineering, some ritual and domestic memory. They are alive, divided and in some cases unaware of one another.

The map also reveals regions where local histories have been standardised or replaced.

The alien crew arrives under orders to leave. Instead, the leader disables the retrieval beacon. The young scientist reaches the interface and stops.

“What do you want to do?”

A distant human settlement answers Earth using a fragment of song.

The approaching force changes course.

---

# PAGE 35 — EPISODE 9: DEPARTURE

## Safety can keep you alive. Connection gives survival meaning.

The crew faces three bad options: keep Earth dormant, surrender the archive, or reconnect human communities and reveal the network.

A domestic recording clarifies the boy’s role. He was never designed as a biological key. He was taught something a machine cannot decide: ask whether people are ready to listen without demanding ownership.

The approaching force demonstrates its method by presenting a subtly altered human story. One ordinary detail has been removed. The boy recognises the manipulation.

Then he finds the fourth option.

Do not send the archive as an object.

Make a new record.

Let people answer it themselves.

---

# PAGE 36 — EPISODE 10: A NEW RECORD

## The first record said: this is who we were. The new one says: answer us.

The finale rejects the idea that humanity can be saved by reducing it to one perfect archive.

The boy and crew assemble a living transmission protocol. Human settlements contribute voices, contradictions, songs, domestic memories, arguments, scientific records and ordinary details. No contribution can become the final version because every message can be answered.

The approaching force can control a central object. A distributed conversation is harder to possess.

The emotional climax is quiet. The scientist asks what the boy wants his first contribution to be.

Not a speech. Something small, human and alive.

Earth transmits.

Across the dark, other lights answer.

---

# PAGE 37 — CHARACTER ARCS

## Everyone arrives believing memory means something different.

**The Boy** begins defended, competent and suspicious of rescue. He ends not rescued but participating, choosing connection without surrendering the right to question and refuse.

**The Young Scientist** begins believing curiosity is inherently benevolent. The scientist ends with moral agency, asking not only what can be known but who has the right to decide.

**The Expedition Leader** begins trusting legitimate protocol. The leader ends choosing accountable protection over institutional permission.

**The Security Officer** begins focused on risk. The officer ends as the crew member most willing to name coercion and abandonment plainly.

**The Crew** begins as observers of a lost civilisation and ends as implicated witnesses inside a living one.

# PAGE 38 — MYSTERY DISCIPLINE

## Answer enough to satisfy. Protect enough to continue.

### Revealed in Season One
- Earth is ecologically alive while large-scale human civilisation is absent.
- The boy was deliberately taught, supplied and concealed.
- Alien command withheld material information.
- The recommended Memory Ark is a distributed cultural-continuity system, not a weapon or biological key.
- Human communities survive beyond Earth in separated forms.
- An approaching power has a pattern of standardising or erasing cultural continuity.
- The boy’s value is living judgement and the right to answer.

### Held for later development
- exact cause and timeline of humanity’s disappearance
- the boy’s final name, carers and full biography
- whether other humans remain physically on Earth
- number and politics of off-world settlements
- complete identity of the approaching force
- final technical architecture of the Memory Ark

Season One answers “Is he alone?” with something richer than yes or no. He is part of a human story that survived in fragments, and for the first time he gets to decide how he enters it.

# PAGE 39 — VISUAL THESIS

## Monumental enough for premium science fiction. Intimate enough that every discovery changes a relationship.

The visual balance is approximately **70% intimate archaeological mystery / 30% epic scale**.

The imagery must feel photographed rather than illustrated. Materials carry age, moisture, weight and optical imperfection. Nature structurally transforms architecture rather than decorating it. Human absence appears through ordinary interrupted detail rather than horror imagery.

No frame should advertise the use of AI.

**Image:** definitive hero still proving friendship, world scale and photographic credibility.

# PAGE 40 — CAMERA LANGUAGE

## The camera observes before it announces.

- large-format wides for geography and awe
- 35–50mm observational framing for relationships and discovery
- 85mm for vulnerable portraits and tactile detail
- low, child-respecting camera height
- slow reveals before action
- motivated natural light and weather
- physical depth rather than synthetic bokeh
- movement only when character or discovery motivates it

Figures may appear small in landscapes, but every wide must lead to an emotional close consequence.

# PAGE 41 — LIGHT AND COLOUR

## Memory is the only thing allowed to glow.

Earth lives in deep wet greens, slate blue, pale stone, aged brick, rust and oxidised copper. Warm sunlight is used sparingly as emotional punctuation.

Alien design uses mineral grey, desaturated blue, oxidised green and bone-neutral layers. Warm amber is reserved for active memory, warning or reply states.

The Golden Record’s gold therefore becomes more than colour. It is a visual signal for invitation, memory and moral choice.

Avoid permanent teal-orange grading, neon cyberpunk and sentimental glow.

**Image:** ten-frame colour progression from wet Earth to distributed memory gold.

# PAGE 42 — ALIEN DESIGN GRAMMAR

## A civilisation that treats memory as a shared public duty.

The alien culture preserves history through living custodians, tactile records and communal interpretation. A record is not considered complete until another person has responded to it.

That principle shapes anatomy, clothing, tools, interfaces, gesture and architecture.

Their forms are layered rather than sealed. Garments reveal repair. Tools retain evidence of previous use. Authority is expressed through responsibility rather than medals. Communication technology is designed to invite reply rather than passive playback.

The cultural contradiction drives the final act: they believe every record deserves an answer, but their institution has decided some truths must remain unread.

**Image:** portrait, silhouette, hand/tool and textile plate.

# PAGE 43 — THE SHIP

## A travelling instrument for listening.

The ship should not resemble a saucer, military carrier or luxury yacht. Its form grows from offset listening and recording structures around a protected inhabited core.

The exterior is asymmetrical but balanced, with no obvious attack-facing cockpit. Receiving surfaces change orientation during approach. Repair history remains visible.

Inside, shared spaces curve around archival structures. Light is local and physically motivated. Personal areas carry objects from previous missions. Command is not a throne.

Emotionally, the same ship changes meaning across the season: impressive on arrival, safe during early friendship, oppressive when sealed layers are exposed, then open again when the boy is allowed to add his own record.

**Image:** exterior and one emotionally legible interior.

# PAGE 44 — TECHNOLOGY AND INTERFACES

## Technology should show consequence, permission and history.

No generic floating blue holograms.

Alien information is displayed as relationships between records anchored to physical surfaces. Uncertainty remains visible. Controls respond to pressure, sequence and orientation. Light follows real channels instead of hovering without source.

The field recorder captures data and interpretation together. The command seal makes secrecy accountable by visibly recording who opened what. The security officer’s primary tool protects space, stabilises structures and restrains without becoming a conventional gun.

The Golden Record is not instantly translated. The crew must listen, compare and infer.

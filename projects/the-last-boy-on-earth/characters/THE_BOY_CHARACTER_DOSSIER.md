# THE LAST BOY ON EARTH
## The Boy — Character Dossier and Survival Logic

**Status:** Development dossier v0.1  
**Purpose:** Provide production-ready character depth for the 50-page Series Development Proposal without converting unresolved development choices into locked canon.

---

## Certainty key

- **LOCKED:** already established by the project canon or approved story structure.
- **RECOMMENDED:** strongest current development route and safe to use in the proposal when clearly labelled.
- **OPEN:** requires founder approval before final canon lock.

This dossier does not change `CANON.md`. It separates what the proposal can state now from what must remain provisional.

---

# 1. Character essence

## One-line character definition

**LOCKED**  
The last known human child: fiercely capable in an empty world, emotionally unpractised with other people, and quietly desperate to learn that belonging does not always end in loss.

## Emotional engine

**LOCKED**  
He wants proof that someone else survived, but the deeper story is whether he can risk loving the people who have already found him.

## Core contradiction

**LOCKED**  
He can navigate a reclaimed city, repair old systems and survive long periods alone, yet ordinary trust, play, apology and dependence feel dangerous to him.

## Audience promise

The audience should never pity him as a passive orphan. They should admire his intelligence, laugh at his social blind spots, worry about the cost of his independence and recognise the universal fear beneath it: if he lets people matter, they may leave.

# 2. Identity framework

## Name

**OPEN**  
The boy's name remains deliberately unlocked pending cultural, market and founder review.

### Naming criteria

The final name should:

- be immediately pronounceable across international markets
- feel human and contemporary without tying the story too narrowly to one decade
- carry warmth rather than heroic destiny
- work naturally when spoken by alien characters
- avoid overt mythological or “chosen one” associations

### Safe proposal usage

Until approved, use **the boy** in editorial copy and **BOY** in production documents. Do not allow temporary names to leak into final page headings, image prompts or motion tests.

## Age

**LOCKED RANGE:** approximately 11–13.

**RECOMMENDED PLAYING AGE:** 12.

Twelve best supports the tonal balance. He is old enough to act with credible agency and practical skill, but young enough that his emotional isolation remains immediately painful and his discovery of a new family retains wonder.

## Origin

**LOCKED:** human, found on reclaimed Earth centuries after humanity's disappearance.

**OPEN:** exact birthplace, family structure, ethnicity, accent and date of birth.

The story should not frame him as representative of all humanity by bloodline. His importance comes from his lived experience, moral choices and relationship to ordinary human memory.

# 3. Recommended biography architecture

## Early life

**RECOMMENDED, NOT FINAL CANON**

The boy spent his earliest years inside a small protected community attached to the terrestrial Memory Ark network. It was not a military bunker or elite laboratory. It functioned as a modest custodial household built around ordinary routines: meals, lessons, repairs, recorded stories and care for the surrounding environment.

He remembers people, but not a complete civilisation. His knowledge of humanity comes from three overlapping sources:

1. fragmentary childhood memories
2. curated lessons left by his carers
3. the vast, contradictory remains of the abandoned world

This gives him authority without making him omniscient. He knows what a kitchen table feels like, but may misremember who sat there. He can identify a football, but may only know the rules from a damaged recording. He understands that a birthday mattered, yet no longer knows the exact date of his own.

## The separation

**RECOMMENDED, DETAILS OPEN**

At some point before the series, the adults responsible for him disappeared from his daily life. The cause should remain emotionally specific but causally linked to the larger mythology.

The strongest route is that they left to protect the Memory Ark's location, draw danger away or reach a failed extraction point. They did not knowingly abandon him for convenience. They expected either to return or for another human contact to reach him.

The boy does not possess the full truth. He has evidence, instructions and conflicting memories. This protects the season mystery and prevents exposition from outrunning emotion.

## Life alone

**LOCKED IN PRINCIPLE; RECOMMENDED IN DETAIL**

He has survived alone long enough to build habits, systems and emotional armour, but not so long that all early human attachment has vanished.

**Recommended duration:** approximately two to four years.

This duration allows him to:

- develop credible survival competence
- experience repeated disappointment and silence
- retain vivid memories of carers
- remain socially recognisable as a child rather than a feral archetype
- plausibly maintain language through recordings, lessons and his own messages

The exact number of years should remain open until the timeline is approved.

# 4. Survival logic

The boy's survival must feel designed, practical and costly. He is resourceful, but he is not surviving through unexplained genius.

## A. Shelter network

**RECOMMENDED**

He does not live in one secret super-bunker. He uses a small network of linked safe places across reclaimed Dublin:

- a primary domestic shelter with emotional history
- a weather refuge near the river
- a high observation point used for signals
- a maintenance room connected to old civic infrastructure
- temporary caches in museums, stations or public buildings

This creates movement, cinematic variety and vulnerability. No single location can solve every problem.

## B. Water

**RECOMMENDED**

Water comes from layered systems rather than one magical source:

- rain collection
- surviving gravity-fed infrastructure
- hand-filtered river or reservoir water in emergencies
- purification materials maintained from old stores

He knows contamination signs through repetition and instruction, not advanced chemistry.

## C. Food

**RECOMMENDED**

His diet combines:

- hardy plants from deliberately tended growing spaces
- preserved emergency stores, increasingly scarce
- simple traps or fishing where appropriate
- edible urban ecology learned through annotated guides and lived practice

Food scarcity should be occasional and specific, not constant misery. The world is fertile, but knowledge and seasonal timing matter.

## D. Power and heat

**RECOMMENDED**

The boy maintains small, local energy systems:

- salvaged solar surfaces
- hand-crank or pedal charging
- low-power batteries
- residual power from hidden civic or Memory Ark infrastructure

He can repair familiar systems by pattern and practice. He cannot casually rebuild advanced technology from first principles.

## E. Health

**RECOMMENDED**

His carers left practical medical instruction, labelled supplies and automated diagnostic tools with limited functions. He understands first aid, infection risk and when he is in danger, but illness remains one of his deepest fears because there is nobody to help.

This vulnerability should surface through behaviour rather than speeches: checking a cut repeatedly, rationing antibiotics he may not need, or hiding symptoms from the crew because dependence frightens him.

## F. Knowledge preservation

**LOCKED BEHAVIOUR; RECOMMENDED SYSTEM**

He records messages for unknown listeners and collects ordinary objects. These are not hobbies added for charm. They are his psychological survival system.

He keeps:

- spoken daily logs
- maps marked with working and failed systems
- object cards describing what he believes things were used for
- names or fragments he is afraid of forgetting
- unanswered transmissions stored by date

The records let him externalise memory and preserve the possibility of an audience.

## G. Why he has not simply left

**RECOMMENDED**

He remains near the protected zone because:

- it contains the only reliable signal and archive access he knows
- his carers instructed him that someone would make contact there
- leaving permanently would feel like abandoning the people who might return
- the surrounding city is the world he understands
- deeper down, waiting protects him from choosing a future without them

His attachment is therefore practical, emotional and thematic.

# 5. Skills and limits

## What he can do

- read terrain, weather and urban danger quickly
- move through overgrown structures with confidence
- repair systems he has maintained before
- improvise tools from familiar materials
- catalogue objects and recordings
- recognise emotional significance in damaged human things
- stay calm during practical emergencies
- notice when alien assumptions about humanity are incomplete

## What he cannot do

- explain the full cause of humanity's disappearance
- operate every hidden system
- understand advanced alien technology immediately
- fight like a trained soldier
- survive indefinitely without infrastructure
- read complex social situations accurately
- accept help without feeling that he has failed
- distinguish every true memory from stories repeated so often they feel remembered

These limits are essential. Competence should generate story, not eliminate danger.

# 6. Psychology and behaviour

## Primary defence

He converts fear into tasks.

When overwhelmed, he checks doors, repairs objects, counts supplies, changes the subject or leaves. Activity lets him avoid naming what he feels.

## Relationship to truth

He dislikes being lied to but withholds information himself. He sees secrecy as survival when he controls it and betrayal when others do. The Memory Ark and sealed-orders story should force him to confront this contradiction.

## Relationship to hope

Hope is not naturally comforting to him. Hope has repeatedly produced waiting, and waiting has produced disappointment. This is why the personal transmission in the middle of the season is both exhilarating and dangerous.

## Relationship to humour

He has a dry, literal wit shaped by old recordings and long conversations with himself. He rarely performs jokes deliberately, which makes his blunt corrections of alien misunderstandings funny.

## Relationship to objects

He does not collect expensive or monumental artefacts. He values objects that imply touch and ordinary life:

- a worn keyring
- a child's drawing
- a cracked mug
- a voice recorder
- a repaired toy
- a handwritten shopping list

This supports the series thesis that civilisation is preserved through lived meaning, not only grand achievements.

## Stress tells

Use consistently across scripts and image direction:

- grips or rubs a small personal object
- answers too quickly when frightened
- refuses food before admitting distress
- checks exits on entering a room
- sleeps close to equipment or doorways
- becomes excessively practical during emotional conflict

## Joy tells

- forgets to monitor his surroundings
- asks several questions at once
- imitates alien gestures without noticing
- shares an object before explaining it
- records a message addressed to a specific person rather than “anyone”

# 7. The personal object

## Recommended object: repaired pocket audio recorder

**RECOMMENDED, OPEN FOR APPROVAL**

A small, heavily repaired voice recorder is the strongest signature object because it unifies character, plot and production.

It can:

- hold fragments of carers' voices
- contain the boy's unanswered messages
- provide imperfect clues rather than clean exposition
- become his stress object
- allow the young scientist to hear private human life
- contribute a living voice to the new Golden Record
- appear clearly in portraiture and close-up visual development

The recorder must remain tactile and limited. It is not an all-purpose plot device, perfect database or hidden supercomputer.

### Alternate objects

If the recorder is rejected, the next strongest options are:

1. a hand-repaired toy with one surviving recorded phrase
2. a ring of labelled keys whose original doors are mostly gone
3. a paper field notebook containing multiple hands and generations of correction

# 8. Relationship arcs

## The boy and the young scientist

### Beginning

He sees the scientist's curiosity as intrusive and naïve. The scientist sees him as the answer to thousands of historical questions.

### Middle

They become equals through mistakes. He teaches context; the scientist gives him permission to be curious about a future rather than only the past.

### Crisis

When the crew's hidden purpose is exposed, he assumes the friendship was another form of study or manipulation.

### Resolution

The scientist chooses him over professional recognition and authority. He chooses to share a private recording, proving that trust is now an action rather than a hope.

## The boy and the expedition leader

The leader initially protects a discovery. Gradually, the leader begins parenting without naming it. Their central conflict is control: both hide fear through responsibility. The final bond is earned when the leader gives him meaningful agency rather than merely promising safety.

## The boy and the security officer

Their early suspicion is mutual. Each recognises the other's survival habits. Trust grows through practical acts rather than dialogue: sharing a watch, returning a confiscated tool, or allowing the boy to lead through dangerous terrain.

## The boy and the ship intelligence

The intelligence has records without lived memory. The boy has lived memory without reliable records. Their relationship embodies the theme of the Memory Ark. Each possesses what the other lacks.

# 9. Season One character progression

## Episodes 1–2: The intrusion

The boy treats the expedition as a threat to his routines and territory. He is capable, suspicious and unwilling to be categorised as helpless.

## Episodes 3–4: The exchange

He begins sharing knowledge when the aliens' misunderstandings become dangerous or absurd. Competence earns him status within the group.

## Episodes 5–6: The temptation of hope

A message addressed to him reopens his belief that a human future exists. Returning home exposes the difference between what he remembers, what he was told and what was deliberately hidden.

## Episode 7: The rupture

The sealed retrieval order confirms his deepest fear: people approach him because he is useful, then make decisions without him. He withdraws before he can be abandoned.

## Episodes 8–9: The choice to be seen

He learns that other humans may survive but are divided and endangered. His decision is no longer simply whether to wait for rescue. It is whether to participate in reconnecting people, knowing visibility brings risk.

## Episode 10: The new record

He contributes his own voice and ordinary memories to a message designed for response. The emotional victory is not that he finds all humanity. It is that he stops speaking only to an imaginary audience and chooses a real, imperfect community.

# 10. Visual character direction

## Silhouette

- compact, practical layers rather than heroic costume
- repaired clothing with visible history
- one recognisable cross-body strap or small field bag
- footwear adapted for wet surfaces and climbing
- no tactical armour, apocalypse cosplay or decorative survival gear

## Face and hair

- emotionally readable at close range
- sun, wind and weather exposure without making him look brutalised
- hair cut or maintained imperfectly by himself
- eyes alert to environment before settling on people

## Wardrobe logic

Every layer should solve a practical problem and imply time:

- waterproof outer layer repaired in more than one material
- softer inner garment tied to his earlier home life
- pockets modified for tools, filters and the recorder
- muted natural colours that sit inside reclaimed Dublin
- one warmer accent associated with memory or hope

## Portrait requirements

The definitive character portrait should include:

- direct but not defiant eye contact
- the repaired recorder visible but not presented like a weapon
- tactile skin, fabric and repair detail
- a simple background that implies the reclaimed city
- vulnerability held inside competence
- no fantasy styling, excessive grime or “chosen hero” pose

# 12. Proposal-ready copy

## Short character paragraph

The last known human child is a survivor by habit and an archivist by instinct. He knows how to read the weather, repair the systems that keep him alive and move through a city reclaimed by nature. What he does not know is how to live with other people. Years of recording messages into silence have taught him that hope can hurt more than loneliness. The alien expedition offers the possibility of family, but accepting it means risking the wound he has organised his whole life to avoid.

## Extended character paragraph

Approximately twelve years old, the boy has spent the most formative part of his childhood maintaining a network of shelters, signals and fragile systems left behind by the people who protected him. He is neither a feral child nor a miniature engineer who can solve anything. His competence is specific, learned and costly. He can cross a collapsing city, identify a contaminated water source and preserve a failing recording, but he struggles to accept a meal he did not earn or admit when he is frightened. He collects ordinary human objects because they prove that civilisation was made from daily acts of care. Across Season One, he moves from speaking to an imaginary audience to placing his real voice inside a new Golden Record, choosing connection even though it may expose him to danger and loss.

## Character hook

**He survived the end of humanity. Now he has to learn how to belong to something new.**

# 14. Continuity rules

- He is a living witness, not a biological key.
- His competence must come from learned routines, tools and infrastructure.
- He cannot know answers the season mystery requires him to discover.
- He should make mistakes under emotional pressure.
- His desire to find humans must sometimes conflict with his growing attachment to the alien crew.
- The story must not punish him for needing help.
- Major emotional change should appear first in behaviour, then dialogue.
- Every object connected to him should carry a specific memory or practical use.
- His visual age, wardrobe repairs, bag, recorder and stress behaviours must remain consistent across all images and motion tests.
